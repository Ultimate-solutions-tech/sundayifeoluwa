The Economics of Value: why people are paid for what they solve, not what they do.

There is a belief that sits quietly underneath a great deal of professional frustration: that working harder should eventually be rewarded. It is a reasonable belief. It is also, more often than not, wrong.

The effort trap

Effort is visible, which is why organisations reward it. Hours can be counted, presence can be observed and busyness can be performed. But effort is not what the market buys. The market buys resolution — a problem removed, a risk reduced, a decision made easier, a cost avoided.

Two people can work identical hours on adjacent desks and produce wildly different outcomes, because one is attached to a problem worth solving and the other is attached to activity that would be missed for a week and then quietly absorbed.

Effort is what you put in. Value is what someone else is better off for having.

Value has a direction

Value only exists in a direction: from your work toward another person's situation. This is why the same skill can be paid differently in different contexts. The skill did not change. The problem it was attached to changed.

  • Scarcity — how difficult it is to find someone who can do this well.
  • Urgency — how quickly the problem costs the person something.
  • Leverage — how many people the work affects once it is done.
  • Trust — how confident they are that you will actually deliver.

Improve any one of those four and the value of unchanged work rises. Ignore all four and no amount of additional effort will move the outcome.

Three questions before you act

Before committing a quarter of your capacity to anything, it is worth answering three questions plainly:

  • Whose problem does this solve, and how much does that problem cost them?
  • What would have to be true for this to matter to them rather than to me?
  • If this succeeded completely, what would be measurably different?

Most projects fail the second question. They are designed around what the builder finds interesting rather than what the user finds valuable — and no amount of execution repairs that misalignment.

What this means for a business

For an organisation, the same logic operates at scale. Positioning is the decision about which problems you will be responsible for. Pricing is the market's answer to how valuable it considers those problems. Sales is the process of helping another person see that value before it has been delivered.

This is why weak positioning produces price pressure, and why price pressure is rarely a pricing problem. It is a value-perception problem wearing a pricing costume.

A working definition

Value creation is the deliberate act of reducing someone else's cost, risk or uncertainty — and being recognised for it. The first half is the work. The second half is the system around the work, and it is the half most people neglect.

Building people. Strengthening businesses. Creating lastingly.
